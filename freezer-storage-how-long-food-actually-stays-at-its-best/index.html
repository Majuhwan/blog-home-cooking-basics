Freezer Storage, How Long Food Actually Stays at Its Best

USDA's official freezer storage chart and refreezing rules, explaining the difference between food that's unsafe and food that's just past its best quality.

Home Cooking BasicsOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

A freezer set at 0°F does something the refrigerator can't: it stops food safety from degrading over time. That single fact confuses a lot of home cooks, because it means the real question for anything in the freezer isn't "is this still safe," it's "is this still worth eating." The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) draws that line explicitly, with a published chart of quality windows for common foods.

Safety and quality are two different clocks

FSIS states it plainly: frozen foods stored continuously at 0°F (-18°C) or below remain safe indefinitely. Freezing doesn't kill the bacteria already present in food, but it stops them from multiplying, which is the mechanism that makes refrigerated food spoil over days while frozen food doesn't spoil on the same timeline at all. The freezer storage times published by FSIS and FoodSafety.gov are explicitly for quality only: how long food holds its texture, flavor, and appearance before freezer burn, moisture loss, or oxidation make it noticeably worse, not a safety deadline.

The official freezer storage chart

Pulled directly from USDA FSIS's published freezer storage chart (items stored at 0°F):

Food Freezer storage (quality)
Bacon and sausage 1 to 2 months
Egg whites or egg substitutes 12 months
Frozen dinners and entrees 3 to 4 months
Gravy, meat or poultry 2 to 3 months
Ham, hot dogs, and lunchmeats 1 to 2 months
Uncooked roasts, steaks, or chops 4 to 12 months
Uncooked ground meat 3 to 4 months
Cooked meat 2 to 3 months
Uncooked whole poultry 12 months
Uncooked poultry parts 9 months
Cooked poultry 4 months
Soups and stews 2 to 3 months
Wild game, uncooked 8 to 12 months

The spread inside a single category is often as informative as the category itself. Uncooked roasts, steaks, and chops span 4 to 12 months depending on cut and fat content, while the same meat once cooked drops to just 2 to 3 months, because cooking changes the fat and moisture structure in ways that make freezer burn and rancidity show up faster.

Why freezer burn happens, and why it's not dangerous

Freezer burn is dehydration and oxidation at the food's surface, caused by air reaching it, usually through loose or damaged packaging or a long stay in a frost-prone section of the freezer. It shows up as grayish-brown, dry-looking patches. FSIS classifies this as a quality issue, not a safety one: freezer-burned food is safe to eat, though the dried or discolored portions can be trimmed away before cooking if the texture bothers you.

Refreezing and thawing rules that are about safety, not just quality

A few parts of FSIS guidance cross back into true food-safety territory, separate from the quality chart above:

  • Thawed-in-the-refrigerator food can be safely refrozen without cooking it first, though FSIS notes there may be some quality loss from the moisture released during thawing.
  • Food thawed by other methods (cold water or microwave) should be cooked before refreezing, since those faster thawing methods let parts of the food sit in the temperature danger zone longer, raising the safety bar for what happens next.
  • The two-hour rule still applies before freezing, meaning perishable food shouldn't sit out more than two hours (one hour above 90°F) before it goes into the fridge or freezer in the first place; freezing doesn't undo time already spent in the danger zone.

A practical freezer routine

Three habits make the chart above actually useful instead of theoretical:

  1. Label everything with the freeze date. Without a date, "how long has this been in here" becomes a guess, and the quality windows above are only useful if you know your starting point.
  2. Freeze in the portion size you'll actually thaw and use. This avoids the repeated partial-thaw-and-refreeze cycle that accelerates freezer burn and texture loss, even though it's not a safety violation on its own.
  3. Rotate older items to the front. Since the clock here is quality, not safety, there's no danger in a forgotten container turning up months later, but rotating stock means you actually eat things while they're at their best instead of defaulting to the newest item every time.

Key takeaways

  • Food held continuously at 0°F or below stays safe indefinitely; freezer storage times are quality guidelines, not safety deadlines.
  • USDA's chart gives specific windows, for example 4 to 12 months for uncooked roasts and steaks but only 2 to 3 months once that same meat is cooked.
  • Freezer burn is a quality problem (dehydration and oxidation), not a safety hazard, and freezer-burned food remains safe to eat.
  • Food thawed in the refrigerator can be safely refrozen raw; food thawed by faster methods should be cooked before refreezing, and the two-hour out-of-fridge rule still applies before anything goes into the freezer.
