Food Safety: How Long Leftovers Really Last

USDA guidance on the two-hour rule, safe refrigeration windows, and reheating temperatures that actually keep leftovers safe to eat.

Home Cooking BasicsOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Leftovers are one of the most common sources of avoidable foodborne illness, not because the original cooking was unsafe, but because of what happens to the food afterward. The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) publishes specific, numeric guidance on exactly how long food can sit out, how long it keeps in the fridge, and how hot it needs to be reheated.

The two-hour rule

According to FSIS, bacteria that cause foodborne illness grow most rapidly between 40°F and 140°F, a range officially called the "Danger Zone." Within this range, FSIS states that bacteria can double in number in as little as 20 minutes under the right conditions.

The core rule that follows from this: perishable food should never sit out of refrigeration for more than 2 hours. If the ambient temperature is above 90°F, such as an outdoor picnic in summer, that window shrinks to just 1 hour. Past that window, FSIS guidance is direct: throw the food away rather than risk it, regardless of how the food looks or smells, since the bacteria responsible for foodborne illness are not something you can detect by sight or smell.

Cooling food fast matters as much as cooling it eventually

One of the most common mistakes is putting a large pot of hot food directly into the refrigerator as one big container. FSIS specifically recommends dividing large amounts of food into smaller, shallow containers before refrigerating, because a big pot of soup or stew takes a long time to cool all the way through, and the middle of that pot can sit in the Danger Zone range for far longer than two hours even while the exterior of the container feels cold.

For large roasts, hams, or whole turkeys, FSIS recommends cutting them into smaller portions before refrigerating for the same reason; a whole roast cools far more slowly in the center than individually sliced or portioned pieces.

How long leftovers actually keep

Once properly refrigerated within the two-hour window, FSIS states that leftovers can be kept in the refrigerator for 3 to 4 days, or frozen for 3 to 4 months. Frozen leftovers remain technically safe to eat beyond 4 months, but FSIS notes they can lose moisture and flavor quality the longer they sit frozen, which is a quality consideration rather than a safety one.

A simple practical habit that follows from this: labeling leftover containers with the date they went into the fridge makes the 3-to-4-day window easy to track, rather than relying on guesswork about how long something has been sitting.

Thawing frozen leftovers safely

FSIS identifies three safe methods for thawing: in the refrigerator, in cold water, or in the microwave. Refrigerator thawing takes the longest but keeps food safe the entire time it is thawing. Cold water thawing is faster but requires the food to be in a leak-proof package, and needs closer attention since it is not a "walk away and forget it" method the way refrigerator thawing is.

Once thawed, FSIS guidance is that the food should be used within 3 to 4 days, the same window as freshly refrigerated leftovers, or it can be refrozen.

Reheating: the temperature that actually matters

When reheating leftovers, FSIS specifies an internal temperature of 165°F or until the food is hot and steaming throughout. This is higher than some of the original cooking temperatures for certain foods, which can be counterintuitive; the reheating temperature is a food safety threshold specifically for leftovers, separate from the minimum safe cooking temperature the food needed to reach the first time around.

In a microwave specifically, FSIS recommends covering the food and rotating it during heating, since microwaves are well known for heating unevenly, leaving cold spots where bacteria can survive even if other parts of the dish reach a safe temperature.

Can you refreeze leftovers that were already frozen once?

Yes, under specific conditions. FSIS states it is safe to refreeze food remaining after reheating previously frozen leftovers to the safe temperature of 165°F. It is also safe to thaw a large frozen container in the refrigerator, remove only the portion you need, and refreeze the remainder without reheating it first, as long as the whole process stayed within the refrigerator (not left out at room temperature) the entire time.

Key takeaways

  • Perishable food must be refrigerated within 2 hours of cooking (1 hour if the ambient temperature is above 90°F), or it should be discarded.
  • Cooling food quickly matters: divide large pots or roasts into smaller, shallow portions before refrigerating so the center cools fast enough.
  • Refrigerated leftovers are safe for 3 to 4 days; frozen leftovers are safe for 3 to 4 months (longer is safe but loses quality).
  • Safe thawing methods are refrigerator, cold water, or microwave; once thawed, use within 3 to 4 days or refreeze.
  • Reheat leftovers to 165°F or until hot and steaming throughout, covering and rotating food in the microwave to avoid cold spots.

None of these numbers require special equipment beyond a food thermometer and a clock. Following them consistently is what actually prevents the kind of foodborne illness that "it looked fine" does not protect against.
