Safe Cooking Temperatures, What the Numbers Mean

USDA's official safe minimum internal temperature chart explained, including why rest time matters and why color is not a safety indicator.

Home Cooking BasicsOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

"Cook until done" is not a food safety instruction, because doneness and safety are not the same thing. The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) publishes a specific chart of minimum internal temperatures, measured with a food thermometer, that applies regardless of how a piece of meat looks on the outside or how long it has been on the heat.

The official temperature chart

According to FSIS and the companion chart published on FoodSafety.gov, here are the minimum internal temperatures for common foods:

Food Minimum internal temperature
Beef, pork, veal, lamb (steaks, chops, roasts) 145°F, plus a 3-minute rest
Ground meats (beef, pork, veal, lamb) 160°F
Ground poultry 165°F
All poultry (whole bird, breasts, legs, thighs, wings, stuffing) 165°F
Egg dishes 160°F
Fish 145°F
Casseroles (meat or meatless) 165°F
Leftovers (reheating) 165°F

A food thermometer is the only reliable way to check these numbers. There is no visual or textural substitute that reliably indicates a food has reached its safe minimum internal temperature.

Why ground meat needs a higher temperature than whole cuts

It is not an accident that ground beef (160°F) requires a higher temperature than a beef steak or roast (145°F). Grinding meat mixes surface bacteria throughout the entire product, rather than leaving it only on the exterior the way it would be on a solid, uncut piece of meat. A whole steak can be seared on the outside and still be safe at a lower internal temperature because any bacteria present were mostly on the surface, which the sear addressed directly. Ground meat does not have that same surface-only distribution, so the higher 160°F threshold accounts for bacteria potentially distributed throughout the product.

The 3-minute rest time is not optional

For whole cuts of beef, pork, veal, and lamb, FSIS specifies 145°F "and allow to rest for at least 3 minutes" as a single, combined requirement, not two separate options. According to FSIS guidance, this rest time matters because the temperature continues to have an effect on the meat during that period, which is part of what makes the overall process safe at that specific temperature threshold. Cutting into the meat immediately after it hits 145°F, skipping the rest, does not meet the same safety standard as the full 145°F-plus-3-minutes combination.

Why color is explicitly not a safety indicator

FSIS states this directly: "color is never a reliable indicator of safety and doneness." This matters because a lot of informal cooking guidance relies on visual cues (pink versus brown, juices running clear) that are not part of the official guidance at all. Meat color can be affected by factors unrelated to safety, including the animal's diet, the specific cut, and even the cooking method itself (some fully safe, fully cooked meat can still appear pink due to factors like added nitrates in cured products or high-altitude cooking effects). A food thermometer removes the guesswork that color inevitably introduces.

Where people usually go wrong

FSIS has noted that a majority of people do not use food thermometers correctly, often checking in the wrong location (too close to bone, fat, or gristle, all of which read differently than the actual meat) or not inserting deep enough to reach the thickest part of the food. For best results:

  • Insert the thermometer into the thickest part of the food, avoiding bone, fat, and gristle.
  • For whole poultry, check the innermost part of the thigh and wing, and the thickest part of the breast.
  • For ground meat patties or loose ground meat dishes, insert from the side into the thickest area.
  • Clean the thermometer probe between uses, especially between raw and cooked foods, to avoid cross-contamination.

Casseroles and mixed dishes follow their own rule

Casseroles, whether they contain meat or are entirely meatless, have their own minimum of 165°F, the same threshold as poultry and reheated leftovers. This single number simplifies a dish that might contain multiple different ingredients (vegetables, grains, sauces, sometimes meat) that would each have different individual safe temperatures on their own; the combined casserole standard of 165°F is the official simplification FSIS provides for these mixed dishes.

Key takeaways

  • USDA's official minimum safe internal temperatures: 145°F plus a 3-minute rest for whole cuts of beef, pork, veal, and lamb; 160°F for ground meats and egg dishes; 165°F for all poultry, casseroles, and reheated leftovers; 145°F for fish.
  • Ground meat requires a higher temperature than whole cuts because grinding can distribute surface bacteria throughout the product, not just on the exterior.
  • The 3-minute rest time for whole cuts is part of the safety standard, not an optional step after reaching 145°F.
  • FSIS states explicitly that color is never a reliable indicator of safety or doneness; a food thermometer is the only reliable check.
  • Insert the thermometer into the thickest part of the food, avoiding bone and fat, since most thermometer-use errors come from incorrect placement rather than a broken thermometer.

A food thermometer costs little and removes the single biggest source of guesswork in home cooking safety. The numbers above are the same ones professional kitchens are held to, and they apply just as directly at home.
